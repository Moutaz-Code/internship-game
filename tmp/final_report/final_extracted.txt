1. Introduction
    Internships are valuable because they require students to convert academic knowledge into decisions
that have real constraints, stakeholders, deadlines, and consequences. My placement at Pixoul Gaming
placed me in a particularly interesting environment: the organization operates a public gaming and
immersive-entertainment venue, while the project I proposed required the mindset of a small game-
development team. I had to think about the attraction of a concept, the realities of building it in Unity,
the operational need for a repeatable arcade session, the safety and comfort of VR, and the evidence
needed before calling an experience successful.
   The internship ran for eight weeks from June 29 to August 21, 2026, with 40 hours completed each
week for a total of 320 hours. I worked under Harout Boyajian, Tech Lead. I did not have a formally
confirmed departmental title, so this report describes the placement accurately as game-development
work under technical leadership and alongside an R&D context rather than assigning an unsupported
organizational label.
    The main outcome was a desktop-first Unity extraction-horror prototype. The game's near-future
nuclear facility provided a focused setting for a short contract: enter, identify dangerous fuel rods,
transport them to the intake, manage an intelligent threat, survive extraction, and leave. The intended
product direction was VR and venue-specific physical immersion, but actual headset and hardware
access was not established during the recorded period. Therefore, desktop implementation and
automated tests served as engineering evidence while XR comfort, physical haptics, and customer
experience remained explicit future validation gates.

1.1 Learning Goals
    My first learning goal was to experience the full path from an early creative idea to a structured,
testable prototype. I wanted to avoid treating game design as a list of attractive features. Instead, I aimed
to define a central player promise, identify the risks that could prevent it from working, choose one
question for each prototype milestone, and use evidence to decide what to keep, simplify, or defer.
    My second goal was to improve my Unity and C# development practice. This included learning how
scenes, prefabs, state machines, deterministic seeds, navigation representations, test assemblies, build
configurations, and debugging tools fit together. A prefab is a reusable Unity asset that can be
instantiated many times with consistent structure [5]. This was especially relevant when I later used a
modular environment kit to construct the P02 facility walls.
    The third goal was to connect architecture to a commercial quality need. Replayability mattered
because Pixoul operates in an arcade-like venue: a one-time experience may be memorable, but a
sustainable game should offer a reason to return. I therefore treated replayability as a quality attribute
that affected objectives, layout states, route choices, threat behavior, reset reliability, testing, and future
content structure. The aim was not unlimited procedural generation. It was controlled variation that
remained understandable, fair, and testable.

3. Internship Activities
3.1 Training and Week 1 Concept Development
    The onboarding period was short but enjoyable. I toured the venue, learned about the different
gaming areas, and researched how the custom experiences combined software with physical equipment.
The most important outcome was not a list of devices; it was a clearer understanding that haptics and
room effects should communicate an in-world meaning. Floor vibration, wind, heat, or a physical
control can be memorable, but if several effects mean the same thing—or one effect means too many
things—the player may become confused.
    My early brainstorming moved through several ideas before reaching a VR horror extraction
roguelike direction. I was attracted to horror because physical effects could reinforce tension, and to
extraction play because it creates a clear job, rising risk, and a satisfying exit. I also explored a heart-rate
sensor as a possible input to a game mechanic. Later risk analysis showed why raw heart rate should not
directly determine detection or failure: sensor noise, individual anxiety, calibration, accessibility,
consent, and positive feedback loops could make the game unfair. The idea was therefore retained as
future research with a simulated fallback rather than claimed as an implemented feature.
   The Week 1 concept was intentionally broad. Details such as the final three-rod objective, bounded
Owl adaptation, three-lobed map, and P02 floor plan were developed later. This chronology matters
because it shows genuine iteration rather than presenting the final design as if it existed from the first
day.

3.2 Week 2 Planning, Business Case, and Architecture
    During Week 2 I converted the concept into a planning spine. I used a business-case and proposal
approach from IT Project Management to convince my supervisor that the project was ambitious but
manageable if it was divided into prototypes. The Sprint 01 plan defined a single question: could a small
VR facility feel tense and understandable while the player located an objective, sensed a threat, and
extracted? The plan also created explicit must-have, should-have, could-have, and won't-have
boundaries [8].
    The Obsidian vault became the center of the planning workflow. The project hub recorded the
current phase, assumptions, and open questions. The vision brief connected Pixoul's venue context to a
15-20 minute contract structure. The backlog separated production, core gameplay, threat, run variation,
hardware, QA, and communication. The risk register documented failure conditions and fallbacks. A
ClickUp-ready task pack translated the plan into actionable work. A first-person design workbook
captured rough ideas about player fantasy, haptic meanings, objectives, the Owl, and the desired reaction
after a run: the player should want to play again.
    Software architecture helped turn replayability into design constraints. The game would not generate
arbitrary runtime geometry. Instead, a deterministic seed would select among authored sockets, route
states, lighting conditions, and bounded threat profiles. A deterministic seed is an input that causes the

player, interaction, session state, objective, threat, run configuration, and hardware seams [9]. This

3.3 Project Definition and Design Objectives
    The resulting game direction was a near-future science-fiction extraction-horror experience in which
a contractor accepts dangerous short jobs. The internship prototype focused on one contract: enter a
damaged nuclear facility, identify three live fuel rods, transport them to an intake, survive the Owl,
complete a timed extraction cycle, and cross the open exit. The contractor fantasy gave the player
agency and a clear reason to be in the facility; the horror came from incomplete information and an
intelligent pursuer rather than from graphic violence.
    The current slice used three active rods because repeated trips created route decisions and escalating
exposure. The Geiger counter supported searching, while scan/acceptance rules distinguished correct
rods. Run variation came from seeded objective placements, route states, threat configuration, and other
authored choices. This produced procedural feeling without the risk of generating broken geometry. The
design also protected rapid reset because an arcade operator needs a reliable way to start the next
session.
    The design pillars were venue-only immersion, dangerous contract fantasy, replayable uncertainty,
an adaptive adversary, and comfort/reliability. These were ideals rather than completed validation
results. For example, the vision targeted a physical effect, but real haptic hardware was unavailable in
the recorded evidence. The report therefore treats the hardware ambition as a design driver and
limitation, not as a delivered feature.

3.4 Game and Systems Design
    The Owl was designed to appear intelligent without depending on machine learning. Its behavior
used a state machine: a controlled set of modes such as patrolling, investigating, pursuing, and
recovering, with explicit conditions for movement between them. It also stored bounded run-local
evidence about player behavior. For example, repeated route tendencies could bias later patrol choices.
The adaptation was deterministic, explainable, capped, and cleared when the run reset. It did not train on
customers, remember players across sessions, or autonomously invent behavior.
    This distinction supported both fairness and engineering. A venue operator and developer need to be
able to explain why the entity chose a route, reproduce a problematic run, and disable adaptation for
comparison. The project therefore kept an operator overlay and seed controls. The player-facing
ambition was that the Owl would pressure repeated tactics while leaving credible counterplay: break
sight, change routes, manage sound, use cover, and create distractions. Automated rules could verify
priorities and thresholds, but only human observation could establish whether these responses felt fair.
    Replayability also influenced content architecture. The broader product direction allowed curated
combinations of sites, objectives, entities, and conditions, but the internship protected one nuclear
contract. Compatibility would be authored rather than assuming every module could combine with every
other module. This reduced combinatorial testing and allowed each physical effect to keep a narrow
meaning. It also demonstrated a key project-management lesson: a scalable concept begins by proving
one complete example, not by constructing a generalized system before its player value is known.

3.5 Weeks 3-4: Unity Vertical Slice and Prototype 1
    Weeks 3 and 4 moved from planning into Unity implementation. The initial vertical slice established
a desktop player, facility greybox, interaction system, fuel-rod objective, intake and extraction states,
threat behavior, run configuration, debugging support, and automated tests. Desktop controls were used
because target XR hardware and runtime were not confirmed. This was a deliberate evidence layer: it
allowed rapid testing of contract logic and navigation without pretending to validate headset comfort or
physical interaction.
    The scene was built as a greybox, meaning a simplified environment used to test size, routes,
interactions, and pacing before final art. Early versions emphasized functional completeness. Later
Week 4 work improved route readability, interactions, environment presentation, fullscreen behavior,
and versioning. A run-local adaptation system was introduced without allowing it to override more
credible information. Sight, sound, route knowledge, and adaptation needed clear priority rules so the
Owl would not appear omniscient.
    Several technical problems became useful learning moments. Movement sometimes disappeared at

had been tested. Changes around XR initialization also needed to preserve whether a rod was currently

    By the end of Week 4, the project had a functioning prototype family and a stronger process for

3.6 Week 5: v0.3 Readable Stealth and Counterplay
    Version 0.3.0.0 narrowed the development question to readable stealth and contractor counterplay.
The milestone strengthened how the Owl interpreted confirmed sight, sound, and last-known
information, and it introduced portable or fixed distractions without allowing a weak stimulus to erase a
more credible target. Graded footsteps and restrained investigation cues were intended to expose cause
and effect through the world rather than requiring a permanent stealth meter [12].
   The delivery was supported by exact recorded evidence: 22 of 22 focused Edit Mode tests, 133 of
133 total Edit Mode tests, and six of six Play Mode tests passed. Unity's Edit Mode tests run in the
Editor and can exercise game and editor code, while Play Mode tests exercise behavior in the runtime
context [4]. The v0.3 Windows development build also completed and launched through its recorded
smoke window. A smoke test is a short check that the application starts and remains alive; it is not a
complete playthrough or proof of experience quality.
    These results showed that documented rules and integration cases behaved as expected at delivery.
They did not show that an uninformed player understood noise, cover, distractions, or the Owl's
adaptation. The human-validation sheet remained open, which is why the report uses “recorded
automated validation” rather than “fully tested.”

3.7 Week 6: v0.4 Spatial Readability
   Version 0.4.0.0 protected the v0.3 gameplay baseline while replacing the map with a stronger spatial
experiment. The selected 7C layout used three unequal lobes around an inaccessible central void, joined
by three pairwise connectors. Each lobe had a different identity and one objective pocket, while the
connectors offered different risk and visibility patterns. The goal was to help the player form a mental
map and make deliberate route choices under pursuit [13], [14].
    The layout was represented declaratively before the scene was generated. In this context, declarative
topology means that rooms, nodes, links, objective relationships, and route requirements were expressed
as data or inspectable rules rather than existing only as manually placed geometry. Automated tests
could therefore ask whether every objective had the required graph-distinct routes, whether connectors
were used correctly, and whether the previous gameplay systems remained integrated.

3.9 Technical Implementation in Unity
    The Unity project used C# components for the player, objectives, interactions, threat behavior, run
configuration, feedback, testing, and editor automation. The architecture favored narrow responsibilities
and interfaces. Session state controlled the current run; seeded configuration selected authored
variations; objective components handled rod and extraction rules; the Owl used a state machine plus
bounded evidence; and feedback or hardware-facing systems sat behind replaceable seams. This reduced
direct dependencies and made it possible to test rules without loading every scene.


                                                        12
    The project used both Unity's NavMesh and a fallback route-graph concept. A NavMesh is a surface
representation used to calculate where an agent can walk. A route graph represents important locations
and connections more abstractly. Structural checks could confirm that navigation data or graph routes
existed, but they could not guarantee that the Owl traversed them naturally in every encounter. This
distinction became more important in P02, where a large manual shell, generated colliders, and later
population work increased the chance that a technically connected route might still be awkward.
    Editor tooling was used when repetitive operations needed to be reproducible and disposable. A
generator could create a known hierarchy, while validation code checked ownership and counts. The
current P02 wall-collider generator, for example, was designed to create separate collider children rather
than editing imported prefab assets. However, its present source and related P02 tests are modified or
untracked, and no current passing test run was recorded during this report audit. Those source assertions
are therefore not presented as completed validation.

failure. Each layer answered a different question, so passing one did not replace the others.

3.11 Documentation and Project Management
    The project used a weekly, question-driven agile rhythm. Planning identified one prototype goal and
the risks that could invalidate it. Implementation and local checks occurred during the week. Evidence,
captures, decision logs, and handoffs were then updated so the next iteration started from a known
baseline. This reduced the chance that a future agent—or I myself—would act on an attractive but
superseded idea.
    Git history provided a dated record of deliverables and manual P02 progress. It also exposed limits.
P01 v0.4 was tracked and build-validated, while P02 remained a mix of tracked manual work, modified
scene/settings, and untracked later tools and captures. For the report audit I treated all working-tree
changes as user-owned and did not reset, clean, stage, commit, push, run Unity, or produce a new game
build.
    Project documentation also supported handoff. The current implementation state, predecessor work,
known risks, facility-kit rules, v0.3 and v0.4 scopes, and human-validation sheets gave later work a clear
evidence boundary. The documents did not make every proposal true; they made it easier to state what
was approved, implemented, automatically checked, observed, or still unknown.

4. Overall Internship Experience
4.1 Technical Skills
    The internship expanded my technical understanding beyond writing individual scripts. I learned to
think about how gameplay state, scene construction, reusable prefabs, run configuration, navigation,
tests, and build settings interact. Problems often appeared at the boundary between systems: a movement
threshold depended on frame timing; an XR initialization change could affect held-object state; a wall
prefab's orientation affected collider ownership; and a graph that was structurally connected could still
produce poor traversal.
    I also learned the value of deterministic and explainable systems. The Owl could have been
described vaguely as “learning the player,” but a bounded run-local model was better for the prototype.
It gave the impression of adaptation while remaining testable and debuggable. The seed system served a
similar purpose by allowing variation without losing reproducibility. These choices made the technical
implementation support the intended player experience instead of becoming complexity for its own sake.
    Manual P02 construction developed a different technical skill. I had to reason about scale, pivots,
angles, wall thickness, doorway dimensions, hierarchy, and modular reuse. The floor plan remained an
authority, but implementing it required interpreting how two-dimensional lines become three-
dimensional occupied space. This work made level design feel less like drawing rooms and more like
managing spatial constraints.

4.2 Professional Skills
    The strongest professional lesson was ownership. My supervisor's trust meant that I could not wait
for every task to be prescribed. I needed to propose the work, explain its value, identify risks, choose a
feasible milestone, and report honestly when evidence was incomplete. The business case and planning
spine helped establish this responsibility at the start of the internship.
    I improved at maintaining traceable decisions. Scope changes were recorded, test counts were
attached to the correct version, and P01 was kept separate from the unfinished P02 workstream. This
discipline matters when a project has many documents and AI-assisted outputs, because polished
wording can otherwise hide that an idea was only proposed. I learned to ask: what is the primary
evidence, when was it produced, who performed the work, and what does it actually prove?
   Another professional skill was accepting limited evidence. It would have been easy to describe
passing tests as proof that the level was readable or the game was complete. Instead, I learned that
reporting an open human-validation gate protects the project. It tells the next developer or stakeholder
what kind of work is required rather than encouraging them to trust the wrong result.

4.3 Soft Skills
   The project improved my communication because the idea had to make sense to both technical and
non-technical readers. I practiced explaining an extraction loop, state machine, deterministic seed,


                                                   16
NavMesh, prefab workflow, and test evidence without relying only on class names. Handoff documents
also required me to state boundaries precisely so another person or agent could continue without
reopening every decision.
    Creative freedom strengthened decision-making. I considered many interesting mechanics—heart
rate, physical heat, wind, floor vibration, broader contract combinations, recovery systems, equipment,
and more—but learned that saying “not yet” can protect the core idea. The risk register gave those
decisions a professional form: a delayed feature was not forgotten; it had a reason, trigger, and fallback.
    The internship also improved patience and problem solving. Manual modular construction involved
repeated corrections that were not glamorous but were necessary for a coherent shell. Technical
debugging sometimes required preserving state, restarting after editor instability, or separating a
configuration symptom from a gameplay defect. These experiences made the final outcome feel earned
through iteration rather than produced by one successful attempt.

4.4 University Applied Knowledge
    Software Architecture and Design was the most useful university course for this project. It taught me
how quality attributes influence architecture and how a niche attribute can still be analyzed
systematically even if it is not a standard textbook example. I applied that approach to replayability. For
Pixoul, replayability was connected to customer return and arcade value, so it could not be treated as a
random-number feature added at the end.
    Replayability influenced the architecture through deterministic seeds, authored variable states,
isolated run configuration, reusable contract concepts, reset behavior, and bounded adaptation. It also
influenced quality assurance: variations needed to preserve a valid critical path and be reproducible
when a defect appeared. At the design level, it encouraged route choice and meaningful uncertainty. At
the project level, it warned against fully procedural geometry and unrestricted module combinations that
could create too much content and testing scope.
    IT Project Management was the second major connection. I used a business case and proposal to
obtain support for the project, then used sprint goals, a backlog, risk responses, priorities, acceptance
criteria, cut lines, stakeholder updates, and versioned evidence to pace the work. These tools helped me
start a large project without pretending that the whole vision could be delivered in eight weeks.

4.5 Career Influence
    The internship made me more certain that I want to continue in game development. I enjoyed the
combination of creative direction and technical structure: deciding what the player should feel,
translating that into systems and spaces, debugging when reality disagreed with the idea, and then using
evidence to decide what changed next.
    The most difficult challenge was replayability and my concern about player retention. It was not
solved by one mechanic, and the internship did not include a customer-retention study. The useful
outcome was learning how to turn the concern into practical architecture and testable decisions. A



                                                    17
repeatable arcade game needs a clear first experience, a reliable reset, visible variation, understandable
rules, and enough uncertainty to make a second attempt meaningful. Future human testing must
determine whether the current prototype actually produces that desire.
   The experience also showed me the type of developer I want to become. I want to be capable of
owning a design problem across code, architecture, level layout, validation, and communication while
remaining honest about work assisted by tools or created by third parties. The project is unfinished as a
product, but it gave me a strong foundation for continuing in technical game development and design.

5. Conclusion
    The eight-week placement at Pixoul Gaming transformed an early interest in venue haptics and VR
horror into a structured Unity prototype and a documented development process. I began with
onboarding and research, used a business case and architecture planning to control an ambitious concept,
implemented and validated successive P01 milestones, and ended the official internship by translating
my own approved floor plan into a mostly constructed manual P02 wall shell.
    P01 v0.4.0.0 is the strongest completed outcome. It preserved the extraction loop and readable-
stealth systems inside a three-lobed facility and has recorded evidence for 28 focused Edit Mode tests,
141 total Edit Mode tests, seven Play Mode tests, a Windows development build, and a 12-second
launch smoke check. P02 is separate: it demonstrates manual level-design and modular-construction
learning, but its tracked completion occurred on August 22 and its later integration remains unfinished.
    The central lesson was that creative ambition benefits from evidence discipline. Tests, builds, logs,
Git history, captures, and handoffs made progress traceable, but none replaced human or headset
validation. The project did not establish player readability, fairness, comfort, physical haptic quality,
heart-rate behavior, or customer retention. These are the correct next questions, together with
completing P02 integration and testing on target venue hardware.
    The internship strengthened my intention to work in game development. It allowed me to connect
Software Architecture and Design, IT Project Management, Unity development, level construction, AI-
assisted workflows, and professional reporting in one project. Most importantly, it taught me to protect
authorship and truth: credit imported assets, describe AI assistance accurately, preserve my own manual
work, attach claims to the right milestone, and state “not tested” when the necessary evidence does not
exist.
